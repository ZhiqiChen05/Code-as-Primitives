01 / The idea

Choose a space of functions
Then search within it

A basketball player first chooses a shooting form: stance, arm motion, release. Repeated shots then tune the release angle, force and timing against where the ball lands. If tuning stops helping, the player changes the form.

The same split is standard in machine learning. A practitioner fixes a hypothesis space \(\mathcal F\) of candidate functions, then runs an optimizer to find \(f^*=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal F}\mathcal L(f)\), where \(\mathcal L\) is the training loss. Here the hypothesis space is not fixed in advance: the model writes it, and rewrites it when the evidence says it is wrong.

In our harness, a frontier model designs the controller's structure, which defines a compact search space of interpretable gains, thresholds and targets. Search then looks for the best parameters within that space. Concretely, the model writes a feedback policy that maps each observation to low-level actions and exposes its tunable parameters as vector. Candidate parameters are tested in batched simulator rollouts by a search algorithm the model designs itself. The resulting trajectories tell the model whether to refine the parameters or revise the structure. The accepted program is then frozen and tested from unseen starting states, with no model in the control loop.

Decide the form of a controller, then its parameters.
02 / The framework

Design the policy
Then search its parameters

Separate the choice of controller family from the choice of numerical values, while allowing new evidence to change either.

Rewriting the whole policy after every rollout leaves many possible explanations for success or failure. A changed threshold and a completely different strategy are both possible edits. Testing a batch within one controller family gives the agent a more comparable set of outcomes.

The harness supports two ways to search. CEM (the cross-entropy method) is a built-in optimizer run by the host. Agentic search lets the model propose candidate batches, inspect their rollouts, and run its own numerical analysis with NumPy and SciPy. It also supplies reference texts for the task category. Computation tools do not themselves access the simulator; candidate evaluations are counted separately.

{·}

Core idea. The learned controller is a program, not a weight vector, so it is inspectable (a file you can read and diff), searchable (its constants are an explicit parameter space), and testable (the same frozen file runs from starts it has never seen).

A running example: a pen in the hand.

Contact-rich control makes both levels matter. In Adroit Pen, the controller sees the hand and pen state and must reorient the pen toward a target. An action that changes the pen’s orientation can also loosen the grasp or eject it, so a useful policy has to turn what it observes into a coordinated change across the fingers and wrist.

We follow one saved run, GPT-6 Astra with Agentic search on the ten-stage random-reset curriculum, through the learning loop. Figure 3 plays it alongside each stage of the loop.

A promising search result still has to pass a frozen-policy evaluation. Once submitted, the code cannot be edited during that evaluation, and the model cannot intervene in individual control steps.

Read the policy and evaluation contract

The core interface is select_action(observation). The program returns native actuator commands or joint-position targets, depending on the environment. In the evaluated policy-only modes, it executes without a model-provider request at each action.

Training acceptance and held-out testing are separate. Training evaluations can feed adaptation. Held-out starts are reserved for frozen evaluation, and their outcomes are not returned to the learning agent.

03 / Held-out evaluation

Freeze the policy
Then test it on unseen starts

The metrics, in full. A policy is accepted once it solves its training starts. It is then frozen and run from starting states it never saw: 30 unseen starts on Adroit (one run) and 50 on DexVerse (two repeats, averaged). Held-out success is the fraction of those episodes that succeed; a task with no accepted policy counts as 0%. Total rollouts is the learning cost before acceptance: parameter-search rollouts plus policy-design resets, the episodes the model spends trying a new controller structure.

We compare two ways of running the search with the same model (Claude Opus 5.5), protocol and held-out starts. In model-driven search, the Agentic search mode of Section 2, the model runs the parameter search itself and is also prompted to design grasp-aware controllers. In CEM search, the host runs the cross-entropy method over PARAMS and the model designs and revises the policy family from the results. A third arm, Baseline (policy search), has not been run yet and is marked TBD. Adroit Pen and DexVerse Long-horizon are discussed in Section 4.

Both searches produce controllers that generalize. Across the 66 DexVerse tasks shown, mean held-out success is 94.4% for model-driven search and 96.0% for CEM search; 49 and 48 tasks respectively score exactly 100%. Per task the two are evenly split: model-driven search is higher on 10 tasks, CEM search on 10, and the rest tie. The gaps are largest in Articulation (81.0% vs. 86.7%), where model-driven search has no accepted policy for OpenHuaweiPhone and neither arm solves OpenGlasses.

On Adroit, both reach 100% on Door. CEM search also reaches 100% on Hammer and Relocate, while model-driven search reaches 93.3% and 66.7%. With 30 starts per task and one training chain per search mode, a single Adroit comparison is a small sample.

Cost is where the two differ. On DexVerse, the median task costs 81 rollouts with model-driven search and 5.5 with CEM search. In 54 of the 66 tasks, the first controllers designed under CEM search pass the training starts after resets alone, without a single search rollout; under model-driven search that happens in 7. Mean cost per task is 154.9 rollouts for model-driven search and 65.9 for CEM search.

The pattern is not uniform. On Adroit Hammer, CEM search spends 1,063 rollouts where model-driven search uses 93, and on DexVerse SqueezeScissors CEM search spends 1,756 to model-driven search's 966 while reaching lower held-out success (68% vs. 99%). A fixed optimizer is cheap when the first design is close; on these two tasks, the model-driven search reached an accepted controller with fewer rollouts.

Scope. These are simulation results with state observations and one training chain per task and arm. Every number comes from the campaign reports, and every video below is a replay of a recorded held-out episode.

The failures are concentrated. Of the 66 DexVerse tasks, model-driven search falls below 90% held-out success on eight: OpenHuaweiPhone and OpenGlasses (not accepted), RelocateObject (58%), FunctionalPourCan (69%), UnscrewCap (78%), PourMugPickUpStick (78%), BimanualLiftBasket (87%) and OpenFlatFolder (88%). Four of them (UnscrewCap, OpenFlatFolder, OpenHuaweiPhone and RelocateObject) are also among the six most expensive model-driven searches.

Cheap acceptance is not the same as a robust policy. FunctionalPourCan is accepted by model-driven search after only 21 rollouts but succeeds on 69% of unseen starts; CEM search reaches 100% on the same task. A short search does not by itself show that the controller is robust.

04 / The difficult cases

Where control is
still difficult.

Pen manipulation and long-horizon tasks expose the cost of coordinating contact, recovery, and multiple stages of behavior.

A benchmark pass can hide a brittle skill.

An earlier fixed-start Pen prototype switched between two control vectors using the pen’s observed axis. On its recorded training start, it accumulated 35 successful frames—enough to pass the aggregate benchmark criterion—then dropped the pen at step 69.

The newer random-reset policy behaves differently in the shown held-out replay: its last 121 frames are continuously successful, and it finishes with the pen retained and aligned. All 30 final held-out episodes end on successful frames.

The earlier prototype passed 2/9 additional seeds; the final random-reset policy passed 30/30 reserved seeds. These are different campaigns, protocols, and sample sizes—not a controlled ablation. The videos illustrate behavior beyond the binary score.

Pen remains expensive to learn. The random-reset run used 6,095 search rollouts and 1,233,120 adaptation environment steps, compared with 875 search rollouts for Hammer.

Long horizons compound the control problem.

DexVerse’s long-horizon tasks combine manipulation and articulation. CookFoods asks the hand to position a pot, place ingredients, pour from two vessels, and operate a stove. OvenBakeSalmon uses two hands to season the salmon, open the oven, load the tray, close the door, and turn a knob.

Completion can depend on earlier conditions still holding: the pot and ingredients must remain correctly placed, or the salmon must remain inside the oven. A controller must coordinate the full task, not just produce an isolated successful motion.

The replays below show MakeCoffee, the long-horizon task that both model-driven search and CEM search accepted. The hand must first lift the mug off its tree, then pour milk over it, set it under the espresso machine, and turn the machine’s switch. Both frozen policies start from the same unseen state, held-out seed 9000, and both succeed.

Lift mug→Pour milk→Place under machine→Turn switch

Both replays use the same held-out seed and camera, at 1× speed. The two controllers move through the subtasks at different paces; both satisfy the task’s strict stage sequence. CEM search reaches similar held-out success after about eleven times as many rollouts.

What these experiments establish

These are simulation results with state observations and one training chain per condition. They do not establish real-robot transfer, robustness to arbitrary disturbances, or superiority over human demonstrations under matched budgets. The evidence supports executable controller acquisition and generalization within the evaluated settings.

05 / Explore the work

The policy is an artifact
you can inspect

The practical output is more than a score: it is code, numerical parameters, and a record of the trials that produced them. Those artifacts let us inspect how a controller responds, reproduce its evaluation, and identify where the next revision should focus.
